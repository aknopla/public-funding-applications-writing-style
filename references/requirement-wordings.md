# Requirement wordings — a phrasebook

A catalogue of how funding bodies actually phrase their requirements, organised by requirement type.

**What this is for.** `SKILL.md` tells you which questions to put to a guideline. This file shows what the answers tend to look like on the page, so you can recognise a clause type from its wording, know which variant you are looking at, and know what that variant obligates. Most of the work of reading a guideline is pattern-matching against a genre you have seen before. This is the pattern set.

**What this is not.** Not a description of any scheme, body or jurisdiction, and not a statement of anyone's current rules. Bodies, schemes, criteria and weightings are omitted deliberately: they change, they close, and a stale specific in a submitted application is expensive. Every wording below is an anonymised example of a *form of words*, not a live requirement. Never quote a figure from this file into an application. Get the current guideline and quote that.

**How to read the quoted phrasings.** Text in quotation marks is representative of wording actually used by funding bodies, with jurisdiction-specific nouns replaced by bracketed generics — `[the jurisdiction]`, `[the companies statute]`, `[the approving officer]`. The shape of the clause is the transferable part.

**Where the evidence is thin.** Some entries end with a *Status* line. These distinguish what bodies publish from what is inferred from panel composition, form design or oversight reports. Do not present an inference to a user as a published rule.

## 1. The shared criterion vocabulary

Across unrelated bodies, sectors and administering departments, a small set of criterion names recurs close to verbatim. A proposal written against one set can usually be re-led to another without new content — **only the emphasis changes.**

The recurring set, in the wording it typically takes:

| Criterion | Common exact wordings |
|---|---|
| The technology or idea | "Innovation and Technology Component" · "Innovativeness" · "Creativity and Innovation" · "R&D content" |
| Whether you can execute | "Technical and Management Capability" · "Technical Capability" + "Management Capability" as separate criteria · "Quality and competence of the management team" · "Capability and track record of the applicant organisation" |
| Whether it will sell or be used | "Commercial Viability of Project Deliverables" · "Commercial Viability of Project Outcomes" · "Market viability with milestones" · "Business scalability" · "Practical application potential of the project results" · "Existence of a Holistic Plan to Realisation/Commercialisation" |
| The money | "Reasonableness of Project Cost" · "Reasonableness of Budget" · "Financial Considerations" · "Reasonableness of Budget and Business Viability" |
| Public benefit | "Relevance in Overall Interest of the Community or Government Policies" · "Relevance with Government Policies or in Overall Interest of the Community" · "Economic Benefits" · "Social and/or Economic Benefits" · "Innovativeness and social impact" · "Social Responsibility" |
| Deliverability | "Implementation Viability" · "Soundness of the [n]-year business plan" · "the viability of the proposal, particularly in terms of its project management" |
| IP | "IP Rights and Benefit Sharing" · "IP Rights" |

Academic schemes use a parallel but distinct set: "academic quality / merit", "institutional commitment", "contribution to academic / professional development", "research impact and potential for social, cultural or economic application", "availability of, and potential for, non-[funder] funding".

Sector schemes substitute a domain criterion at top weight while keeping the rest of the frame — an environmental fund's "Potential Contributions to Decarbonisation and Environmental Protection", a film fund's "creativity and quality of the film screenplay", an incubator's "Functional prototype or product secured by design to solve a real problem".

**Use.** When a criterion name is unfamiliar, find which row it sits in. That tells you what content it wants, even if the body has never explained it.

## 2. Scoring instruments

Three instruments appear, and which one a body uses changes how you allocate length.

### 2.1 Published percentage weightings

The most tractable case. Weights are stated per criterion and sum to 100.

**Weighting reveals intent, and is the single most useful sentence in any guideline.** Three patterns worth recognising:

- **The same criteria reweighted by stage of maturity.** One body runs an identical seven-criterion frame across an early-stage and a mature-stage variant of the same scheme. Early stage loads roughly two-thirds of the weight onto the technology and technical capability; the mature variant redistributes it toward commercialisation and financials. Same seven names, different brief.
- **Emphasis inverted between sibling schemes.** Two schemes in one family, near-identical criterion lists: one puts its largest single weight on the physical thing being built, the sibling puts the same weight on social and economic benefits instead. A proposal moved between them without re-leading will read as answering the wrong question.
- **Weights swapped between stages of one scheme.** A staged scheme weights technology above commercial viability at first stage, then exactly reverses the two at second stage. The same project must be argued differently depending on which stage it is entering.

A fourth pattern: **weightings that vary by grant size band**, with the quality criterion falling and the application-potential criterion rising as the requested amount goes up. Larger asks are scored more on downstream use.

### 2.2 A published rating scale instead of weightings

Common in academic schemes, which often state explicitly that they do not use percentage weightings. The scale carries the discrimination instead. Descriptors run along these lines:

> "Outstanding and internationally excellent… highest priority for funding"
> "Very high international standards… funded as a matter of priority"
> "High international standards… worthy of consideration for funding"
> "Good international standards but… not of sufficient priority"
> "Adequate qualities but not internationally competitive"
> "Some strengths but major weaknesses and flaws"
> "Numerous and significant weaknesses and flaws"

— usually with a stated cut-off below which nothing is recommended.

Where a scale like this is used, a criterion is often nominated as dominant in a specific, limited way: **"academic merit is the overriding criterion"** *when other factors are of equal weight*. That qualifier matters. It is a tie-break rule, not a licence to under-serve the other criteria.

### 2.3 An unweighted list of factors

The common case for intermediaries and for discretionary schemes. The guideline lists factors "the [approving officer] will consider", often lettered (a)–(j), with no weights and no scale. Such lists usually close with a catch-all:

> "any other special factors which are relevant"

Treat an unweighted list as equally weighted until the form tells you otherwise — and then let the form tell you otherwise. See `SKILL.md`, *Step 1*: section order and allotted space are the real weighting.

Assessment principles, rather than criteria, are a related form — stated as sentences about what the project should do:

> "the project should lead to immediate or long-term business development of the applicant enterprise"
> "the project should have good prospects of improving the competitive advantage"
> "the project should have concrete deliverables to facilitate progress monitoring"
> "the project should include solid actions for developing business" in the target market

Each of these is a criterion. Write a section that answers each one in its own words.

### 2.4 Hurdles — the clauses that kill strong proposals

Separate from and more dangerous than the weighting. Published forms:

> "applications must pass all vetting criteria to obtain approval and funding"
> applicants must achieve "at least half the weighting in each category" or face rejection
> a rating "of 3 or below" is "not recommended for funding"

Note also the *absence* pattern: oversight reviews have found bodies operating with **no consistent passing mark at all**, different administering units setting their own thresholds — anywhere across a wide band — or setting none, and with **no per-criterion minimum** defined. So a hurdle may exist in practice without being published, and may be applied inconsistently. Either way the defence is the same: no criterion gets a thin section.

**Compliance items scored as merit.** Watch for a disclosure requirement sitting inside the assessment criteria rather than in the terms and conditions — for example "full disclosure of the identity of all shareholders and of its holding companies (up to the ultimate parent company level)" listed as one of five assessment criteria, or a scored "IP Rights and Benefit Sharing" criterion, or a criterion scoring the **vendor's** record rather than the applicant's:

> "adverse record of consultants and/or service provider(s)"

Where compliance is scored, write the annex as an argument, not a form.

## 3. Eligibility clauses

### 3.1 Legal form — two formulations that must not be conflated

> **"incorporated in [the jurisdiction] under [the companies statute]"** — the stricter test. Excludes sole proprietors and partnerships.
> **"registered under [the business-registration statute]"** — broader. Admits unincorporated forms.

Some schemes accept any of several: incorporation, business registration, or statutory-body status. Others narrow hard in the other direction — lead applicant restricted to named research institutions, to "designated local public research institutes", to "local non-profit-making organisations", or to a closed list of named universities. A parallel formulation appears in incubators: "technology start-up company limited by shares".

Read the exact noun. "Registered" and "incorporated" are different obligations with different evidence.

### 3.2 Negative eligibility

> "not a listed company in [the jurisdiction]"
> "not a government subvented organisation or subsidiary of any government subvented organisation"

### 3.3 The local-operations test

> "substantive business operation in [the jurisdiction]"

Designed to exclude shell registrations. Where a body defines it, the definition is usually an annex listing the factors assessed — typically **nature of business, scale, investment, employee count, customer information, year of establishment, and tax assessment status.**

**This is worth singling out.** Where a body publishes rejection reasons at all — and almost none do — failure to evidence substantive local operations is routinely among the leading ones. It is a compliance failure, not a merit failure, and it is cheap to fix with dated documents. See `SKILL.md`, *Clearing stated requirements*, for the sentence pattern.

A second published rejection reason in the same family: **inability to obtain the licences required in the target market.** Regulatory feasibility is assessed even where no criterion mentions it.

## 4. Benefit-to-the-jurisdiction clauses — four distinct obligations

These look interchangeable and are not. Identify which one you are under.

**(a) As a scored criterion.** "Relevance in Overall Interest of the Community or Government Policies" and its variants (§1). Obligation: argue it in the narrative, at a length proportionate to its weight.

**(b) As a geographic work requirement.** A stated minimum share of the work performed locally:

> "the majority of the [R&D] work of a project should be conducted within [the jurisdiction]"

— often with a permitted percentage elsewhere and anything beyond it requiring prior approval and written justification. Sector variants get specific: a minimum share of below-the-line production expenditure incurred locally; named roles required to hold local residency. Obligation: a work-location breakdown, and approval sought in advance for any excess.

**(c) As an asset-retention condition.** Funded equipment or facilities may not be transferred out of the jurisdiction for a defined lock-in period without prior approval. Obligation: post-award, and it binds for years.

**(d) As a public-benefit test.** The sharpest form, and easy to fail without noticing:

> benefits must serve "individual… sectors or the entire… industries as a whole, **and not just an individual private company**"

Obligation: the beneficiary must be the sector, not you. A proposal written as a company growth plan fails this even if every other criterion is met. Where this clause appears, restructure the benefit argument around sector-level outcomes.

A milder variant ties benefit to the applicant's *local* operations specifically, rather than to the applicant generally.

## 5. Matching contribution clauses

Ratios vary; the forms are stable.

- **Equal cash matching** — the applicant contributes "no less than half of the total project cost", in cash.
- **Applicant-majority or funder-majority matching** at a stated ratio.
- **Third-party sponsorship as a threshold** — the scheme requires sponsorship from industry at or above a stated share of project cost, sometimes with a lower floor available by exception and approval. Sibling schemes in one family may require none, a moderate share, or a majority.
- **Staged matching** — different ratios at first and second stage of the same scheme.

**In-kind rules are where applicants lose money.** The most explicit published formulation allows in-kind to include "donation of equipment and consumable that is exclusively used for carrying out the project", and then expressly excludes:

> "Discount in purchases, time cost of equipment, manpower costs and consultancy services"

That exclusion list is close to standard across the genre even where it is not printed. Assume your own staff time, your own equipment, and any supplier discount do **not** count as contribution unless the guideline says in terms that they do.

Committed sponsorship is typically required to be evidenced — actually received and documented — before the final instalment is released.

## 6. Intellectual property clauses

**The default is that IP vests with the applicant.** Common wordings:

> "as a general rule, Intellectual Property Rights generated from the Project should be fully vested with the Applicant"
> "IP rights generated from the project should be vested with the lead applicant"
> "IP rights generated from the project should be vested with the Recipient Organisation"

The variations that matter sit around that default:

- **A licence back to the funder.** Ranges from a licence over project *reports* only, to a full "non-exclusive, perpetual, irrevocable, royalty-free worldwide licence" that the funder "may be requested" to be granted over arising IP. Read which.
- **Disclosure of arising IP** as a standing obligation, separate from ownership.
- **Background third-party IP** requires evidenced consent or licence, and typically an indemnity to the funder.
- **Contribution-linked ownership**, where a collaborative scheme sets ownership by relative contribution and requires it documented in a project agreement **executed before commencement**.
- **A benefit-share floor** guaranteeing the project team a stated minimum share of benefits arising from generated IP.
- **IP as a scored criterion** (§2.4). Where present, the IP section is persuasive writing.

## 7. Deliverables language

Near-universal phrasing, and worth templating because the same words recur across unrelated bodies:

> deliverables in **"concrete and measurable terms"**, with "expected quantitative and qualitative outcomes" and "measurable parameters indicative of the functions, purposes and performance"
> "concrete deliverables and outcomes"
> "concrete deliverables to facilitate progress monitoring"

The consistent demand is a **parameter**, not a description. "An improved process" is not a deliverable; "a process validated at ≤ X ppm across N production runs, documented in a test report" is.

This is also the clause that makes hedged future scope costly — see `SKILL.md`, *Scope discipline*.

## 8. No-double-funding clauses — two very different bars

**The expenditure bar** — the common form. The same cost may not be funded twice:

> "no double subsidy is allowed for the same part of an expenditure item"
> expenditure "already funded by [the government] … a subvented body/institution or a university shall not be covered by the Project Funds"
> expenditure items "shall not receive funding support from other local public funding sources"
> a declaration that the measures "have not and will not" receive other funding

**The concurrent-submission bar** — stricter, and the one that catches people:

> "applicants must not submit the same proposal to more than one funding scheme, either concurrently or before the final result is issued"

This bars the *submission*, not the payment. Under it, a portfolio strategy of applying widely and accepting the first offer is a breach.

Related forms:
- **Application quotas** — caps on how many applications one named investigator may submit per round, and how many concurrent projects one organisation may hold.
- **Disclosure with an exception route** — declare what has been sought elsewhere; overlapping elements are excluded *unless* the committee recommends an exception.
- **Full-portfolio disclosure** — disclose all funding applications, admissions and grants received or anticipated, extending in some cases to similar projects of the **founders and shareholders personally**, not only the applicant entity.
- **The time-commitment table** — the academic form. Rather than a yes/no declaration, a mandatory table covering completed projects over a stated recent period from all sources, all ongoing projects, all pending proposals, and "related research work" with **no time limit**, expressly including "data collection, preliminary research, working papers, publications… presentations, media interviews". A second table repeats it for co-investigators.

## 9. Duration, budget structure and disallowables

### 9.1 Duration

Stated as a ceiling, with the qualifier carrying the flex: **"generally not exceed"**, **"normally be completed within"**, or a flat cap. Typical published ceilings across the genre run from about 12 months for adoption-type schemes to 24–36 months for R&D, up to five years for large institutional research, with staged schemes stating both a per-stage and a total cap. Some state **no extensions permitted** in terms; others require prior approval beyond a stated slippage.

A minimum may also apply — engagement-type schemes commonly set a minimum duration per placement, a cumulative cap, and a bar on overlapping engagements.

### 9.2 Budget categories

The dominant structure is tripartite plus overhead:

> **Manpower** · **Equipment** · **Other Direct Costs** · **Administrative overheads**

Overhead is capped, typically as a stated percentage, with the *base* of the percentage varying — of funding requested net of overheads, or of total approved project cost. Read which; they differ materially.

### 9.3 The clause that rejects budgets outright

> unspecified items "such as miscellaneous, sundry and contingency etc. will not be accepted"

Every line must name a thing.

### 9.4 Allowable / disallowable lists

Where published, these are detailed and closely similar across bodies. A representative composite:

**Manpower — allowed:** salaries of project staff for time on project activities; statutory retirement contributions.
**Manpower — disallowed:** sales commissions, bonuses, contract gratuities, annual adjustments, fringe benefits, housing, education, training, medical, insurance, severance, overtime; and emoluments for anyone already on a government or university payroll.

**Equipment — allowed:** renting, procuring or repairing equipment specifically for the project, with **title held locally by the applicant**.
**Equipment — disallowed:** use charges for equipment the applicant already owns, depreciation provisions, general office and IT equipment.

**Other direct costs — allowed:** outsourcing to third-party vendors; consumables; prototypes and samples for functional verification (*not* for commercial production); standards and compliance testing by certified laboratories; testing and certification; industrial design; trials; patent registration fees; audit fees; market research, sometimes only at the approving officer's discretion.
**Other direct costs — disallowed:** premises (rent, renovation, operation, repair), utilities, transport, conference and travel, general administration, staff training, entertainment, advertising, trade mission fees, non-project service charges, prior-period adjustments, capital financing costs.

### 9.5 Manpower rate benchmarking

Oversight reviews have identified "large variations in the monthly salaries of similar proposed project posts" with no guidance on what is reasonable. Consequence for the applicant: salary levels are a live scrutiny point, and an unexplained rate invites a clarification round. State what each rate is benchmarked against.

## 10. Related-party and procurement clauses

Structurally the most distinctive block in the genre, and where post-award trouble concentrates.

### 10.1 Defined-term restrictions

The fullest published form defines a term — **"Restricted Persons"** or equivalent — to cover the applicant, its associates and associated persons, shareholders, directors, current employees, and their related parties. Then:

- **Procurement** from a Restricted Person requires **prior approval** from the approving officer.
- **Hiring**, "with or without remuneration", of associates, associated persons, close friends of shareholders or directors, or related parties of suppliers, requires prior written approval — via a named **engagement declaration form**.
- **Shareholder and director remuneration**: "in general, company shareholders/Directors shall **NOT** take any form of remuneration from the Project Funds." Where one serves as project coordinator or team member, a separate declaration form with written justification and conflict mitigation is required.
- **Supplier interest disclosure**: any supplier with a direct or indirect interest must be disclosed in full, with proposed mitigation, **before** the contract is made.

A blunter version appears in simpler schemes:

> applicants "must not engage a consultant/service provider/supplier whose owners, shareholders, management are the owners, shareholders, management of the applicant or their relatives"

— sometimes with an added bar on the supplier providing **loans** to the applicant.

### 10.2 Quotation and tender thresholds

Tiered by value, escalating through: no written quotation required → two quotations → three → five → **open competitive tender** at the top band. Tender documents are typically required to carry a **"probity clause, an anti-collusion and anti-bid-rigging clause"**, sometimes with templates supplied as an annex.

### 10.3 The cross-scheme baseline

Where a jurisdiction has an anti-corruption agency, it commonly publishes a **grantee's integrity and accountability guidebook** that multiple schemes cite by reference. Where a guideline points to such a document, it is incorporated into your obligations. Find it and read it.

## 11. Declarations

### 11.1 By the applicant

- **Beneficial ownership** — "full disclosure of the shareholder(s)' identity and holdings (up to the top level)", i.e. through intermediate holding companies to ultimate parent.
- **Conflicts with nominated assessors** — where a scheme requires the applicant to nominate external experts (below), any relationship with a nominee must be declared.
- **Concurrent workload** — whether the project coordinator or team members "will work on more than two projects within the same project period". Oversight reviews have found this question answered loosely and not carried into assessment reports; expect tightening.
- **Anti-bribery compliance**, sometimes extending to compliance with **foreign** anti-bribery law for overseas operations.
- **Public-order or national-security compliance** — newer and spreading. Appears three ways: as a general undertaking to conform with all legislation; as an explicit **scored** assessment factor ("likelihood of compliance with… including national security provisions"); and, most strikingly, as **Section A of the application form** — a standalone agreement before any substantive question.
- **Use of AI** — recent, and expect it to spread. The fullest published form requires disclosure of "models / systems / tools being / to be used, purpose of usage, how and the extent of such AI usage."
- **Data collection and management** — typically four components: collection and consent procedures, data security, sharing controls, and compliance with the applicable data-protection statute.
- **Ethics and other approvals** as a dedicated form part, with discipline-specific add-ons (sample size and power estimates; a separate trial protocol).

### 11.2 The lobbying bar

Direct and absolute, and it voids applications:

> "applicants must not contact Committee members directly"
> applicants "should avoid approaching [committee] members to discuss applications"

— with offering any advantage an offence under the anti-bribery statute that "renders applications null and void."

### 11.3 By the assessors — useful to know, because it shapes who reads you

- Members declare whether they are "directly or indirectly related to an application" and are "refrained from participating in the discussion of that application"; larger bodies require **annual** declarations plus per-application recusal.
- Peer-assessment bodies publish additional safeguards: conflict-free assignment, an **"objective random process" for examiner selection**, confidentiality and IP protection, and a code of conduct.
- Academic schemes require **external reviewers to self-declare expertise level** — typically "expert", "somewhat familiar", or "not knowledgeable" — with those declaring the lowest level **barred from proceeding**, and those with "major conflicts of interest" excluded. Some operate a **blacklist** for reviewers who return insufficient substantive commentary.
- Applicant-declared conflicts do not necessarily count against you: one published assurance states that declared conflicts "will not adversely affect the application", with the conflicted panellist recusing instead.

## 12. Reporting and audit clauses

**Reporting cadence is usually duration-triggered.** A representative rule: projects at or under a stated length file a final report and audited accounts within a short window of completion; longer projects additionally file a progress report and annual audited accounts at a stated interval.

**Audit requirements are specific about the auditor, not just the audit:**

> an auditor "approved by the Government", registered under [the professional-accountants statute] and holding a current practising certificate
> an auditor independent and registered under [the financial-reporting statute]
> auditors must follow the administering body's published **"Notes for Auditors"**

Common attached rules: **accrual basis**; a defined account set (statement of income and expenditure, balance sheet, notes, auditor's report); **audit fee caps banded by project size** and by which report it is; and **record retention for a long fixed period — seven years is typical.**

Some schemes require audited accounts only above a grant threshold, accepting project reports and receipts below it.

Deliverable sets beyond the report itself commonly include presentation slides, progress reports, a final report, audited accounts, and dissemination through publications or events.

## 13. Format and language clauses

### 13.1 Two format regimes

**The loose regime** — business-facing schemes commonly publish **no word or page limits at all** for narrative sections. Absence of a limit is not permission to be long: with no limit, the form's section structure and the reviewer's patience are the only constraints, and length must be allocated by weighting.

**The strict regime** — academic schemes publish exhaustive schedules. A representative full spec:

> Global: [serif face] 12pt, 2.5 cm margins all round, single-line spacing.

| Section | Published limit |
|---|---|
| Resubmission statement | "maximum of 500 words" |
| Keywords | "maximum of five keywords (a maximum of 30 characters per keyword)" |
| Project abstract | "maximum of 400 words… comprehensible to a non-specialist audience" |
| CV and publication list | "two sides of A4 in pdf… or a maximum of 800 words" |
| Project team and collaboration plan | "800 words maximum" |
| Project objectives | "maximum of 800 words", bullet-point format |
| **Main project statement** | **"maximum of 10 A4 sides"** total, subdivided: |
| — context, questions, methods | "maximum of seven A4 sides in total" |
| — timetable / Gantt chart | "a maximum of one A4 side" |
| — diagrams and photos | "a maximum of two A4 non-text sides" |
| **Impact statement** | "maximum of two A4 sides", "comprehensible to a lay person" |
| References | "up to three A4 sides maximum", standard citation format |
| Facilities and major equipment | "maximum of 400 words" |
| Appendices (education plan; technology transfer plan) | "one side A4" each |

Note what the subdivision does: it **guarantees** space to the timetable and the figures, and caps the prose. Under a regime like this, the page budget is the outline.

### 13.2 Form structures

Business-facing forms commonly run lettered parts along these lines: applicant details → project description → **justifications against the assessment criteria** → implementation plan and milestones → optional benefit-sharing → declarations and disclaimers. The third part is the point: **the form is structured by the criteria**, one section per criterion, which removes all guesswork about allocation.

Research-fund forms front-load compliance: a public-order agreement, then organisational information, then the proposal, budget, assessor nomination, declarations, document checklist, confirmation.

Incubator and business-plan forms mandate components rather than criteria — typically: executive summary (mission, vision, objectives) → product or solution → R&D content and plan → sales and marketing plan (customers, market size, competition, pricing) → core team (org chart, backgrounds, manpower projections) → financial plan (multi-year projections, fundraising, profitability) → **alignment with the programme's own value-add.** That last component is the intermediary's mandate showing up as a form section; see `SKILL.md`, *Writing for an intermediary*.

**A visual executive summary is sometimes mandatory** — for instance a six-slide deck submitted as PDF or image alongside the narrative form. Where required it is functionally the lay-reader device: it is what a panel member outside your domain will actually look at.

**Required annexes** to watch for: letters of collaboration specifying each collaborator's "contribution… time spent, role and responsibilities"; confidentiality authorisations; statutory declarations for named shareholders and directors; a document checklist that is itself a form part.

### 13.3 Language

- **Either-language** is the norm for business-facing schemes: "the application form is available in [language A] and [language B] and may be completed in either."
- **Single-language mandates** occur, typically in academic schemes, sometimes with a second-language abstract required only *after* award, for public access.
- **Bilingual within one application** — rarer, easily missed, and expensive to discover late: key elements must be articulated in **both** languages, not either.
- **A prevailing-version clause** — where versions conflict, one named language governs. Which one determines where translation risk sits.
- **Language may simply not be specified**, particularly by intermediaries. Ask.

## 14. Process and finality clauses

### 14.1 The standard pipeline

Near-universal across administering bodies, whatever their sector:

**secretariat compliance screen → external technical review → committee or panel scoring, often with a presentation → a single named approving officer decides.**

Two things vary, and both matter:

- **External review is discretionary in most schemes and mandatory in academic ones.** The discretionary form reads "may be subject to external review", "external expert consultation on technical aspects", or "review by relevant [departments] and external reviewers" — triggered by technical complexity. The mandatory form specifies a **minimum number of reviewers per proposal**, often requiring them to be independent and non-local.
- **Who decides.** In most schemes the committee **recommends** and a named officer **approves** — a controlling officer, a commissioner, a permanent secretary. Some place final approval with a committee instead. Discretionary funds may reserve the decision "at [the government's] absolute discretion" after considering a recommendation.

**Applicant nomination of assessors** is a distinctive variant: above a threshold grant size the applicant must itself nominate a set number of external experts, with a double-blind arrangement below the threshold and disclosure of investigator credentials to assessors above it.

**Panel composition shapes what you write.** Two archetypes:
- Mixed panels seat technical experts alongside **ex-officio representatives of the relevant policy department**. Consequence: the "relevance to government policy" section has a specialist reader in the room who is *not* a technologist, and thin policy sections get noticed.
- Pure peer-review panels exclude government and industry entirely, appoint members **"ad personam"** so they do not represent their institutions, and may appoint non-local chairs.

Large membership rosters generally operate as a **pool** from which smaller panels are convened per round, with a stated quorum, rather than as a body that sits together. *Status: this is usually not published; where it is visible at all it is via oversight reports. Treat as likely, not certain.*

### 14.2 Deemed withdrawal — the clock clauses

The genre's most avoidable loss mode, because it has nothing to do with the proposal. Published forms:

> failure to respond to secretariat questions "within a reasonable time frame will deem the application to be unsuccessful **without further notice**"
> clarification requests must be answered within a stated short window — **two weeks is a published example** — or the application "may be treated as withdrawn"
> recommended applicants must address panel comments and submit the finalised application within a stated period — **three months is a published example** — or the proposal "will be considered to be withdrawn"

Note the third: it applies **after** you have been recommended for funding. Applications are lost at that stage.

### 14.3 Finality

> "the Committee's decision is final and no appeal shall be entertained"
> "appeals are not considered"
> "the decision from the Assessment Panel for each application shall be final and binding"

Assume no appeal. The occasional exception is worth knowing about: some peer-assessment bodies offer a **review within a short window** — 30 days is a published example — while **expressly excluding challenges grounded in the judgment itself** (artistic merit, academic quality). So the review covers process, not score.

### 14.4 Resubmission as a designed process

Where permitted, it is a full reassessment with specific requirements:

- Declined applications may be resubmitted **"if substantially revised"**.
- The resubmission must address the stated rejection reasons with additional evidence.
- It is treated as a **new application undergoing full reassessment**.
- "The applicant should set out clearly the differences between the resubmitted application and the previous one."
- **Inadequately revised submissions are returned without processing.**

Academic schemes formalise this further: applications are classified **new / resubmission / continuation**, with a dedicated **resubmission statement** under a word cap addressing prior reviewer feedback, and guidance to allow adequate revision time between cycles.

A related warning worth recognising: guidance against **"breaking awards into isolated components"** rather than pursuing genuinely integrated work. Salami-slicing a programme into multiple applications is visible to a panel and is called out.

### 14.5 Processing times

Published oversight findings put combined processing at roughly **five to nine months** from submission to decision for research schemes, with the review noting that long processing "might dampen the interest of researchers, discourage support of the industry, delay the commercialisation of deliverables". Plan the applicant's cash flow and project start date against the slow end.

## 15. Lay-accessibility clauses — what is actually required

The clauses that bear directly on `SKILL.md`'s core method. **Explicit requirements are rarer than you would expect.**

### 15.1 Published, explicit

Academic schemes provide the strongest published language, and it is unambiguous:

> the project abstract must be **"comprehensible to a non-specialist audience"**
> the impact statement must be **"comprehensible to a lay person"**

The structural reason is visible in the same guidelines: a proposal goes to assigned **first and second readers** on a subject panel who may not be sub-field specialists, *plus* external reviewers who have self-declared as either "expert" or merely "somewhat familiar". The document is therefore designed to be layered — a lay-accessible abstract and impact statement wrapped around a technically dense core. That is the two-register method, mandated by the form.

Impact is typically defined for you, and the definition is a content checklist: **"demonstrable contributions, beneficial effects, valuable changes"** beyond academia — across economy, society, culture, policy, health, environment or quality of life — assessed on **"reach and significance"**, with explicit relevance to the jurisdiction.

### 15.2 Implicit but inferable

Elsewhere the requirement is real but unstated, and shows up in the design rather than the text:

- **A mandatory visual summary** (§13.2) exists to serve panel members outside the technology domain.
- **Published FAQ guidance** on what assessors want commonly asks for "a clear description of what the project aims to develop" with **novel elements and underlying scientific principles stated** — i.e. state the principle, do not assume it.
- **Presentations and Q&A are common** across grant schemes, incubators and the full-proposal stage of research schemes. Content must survive being spoken and questioned, not just read.
- **Panel composition is itself the guidance** (§14.1).

> **Status.** No published style guide, readability rule, or explicit lay-writing instruction was found outside the academic schemes quoted in §15.1. Everything in §15.2 is inference from panel composition and form design. Present it to a user as such.

### 15.3 What assessors say they look for, per criterion

Where a body publishes this (usually in an FAQ rather than the guide), it is close to a content spec:

- **Innovation / technology** — a clear description of what the project aims to develop; the novel elements; the underlying scientific principles. Some schemes state a positioning rule directly, e.g. that **upstream research receives lower priority than commercialisable solutions** — which inverts how the same content should be led.
- **Technical and management capability** — the ability of the named coordinator and team "to deliver the proposed project fully"; relevant experience; feasibility.
- **Cost** — benchmarking against industry standards; itemised breakdowns; manpower rates; evidence that expenses align with scope.
- **Commercial viability** — target customers; market potential; competition strategy; a credible commercialisation plan with revenue projections.
- **Community and policy relevance** — societal benefits; alignment with stated government initiatives; benefit-sharing where offered.

## 16. What oversight reviews find — and what it means for an applicant

Independent audits of these schemes are published in some jurisdictions, and they are the most candid material available about how assessment actually behaves. Recurring findings, with the applicant-side consequence:

| Finding | Consequence for the applicant |
|---|---|
| **Optional fields go unfilled.** One review found only 4 of 10 examined applications supplied team qualifications and experience — despite team capability being a published, scored assessment factor and the field being optional. | Fill every optional field. It is the cheapest differentiator in the genre. |
| **Rejection feedback is thin.** Unsuccessful applicants "were only briefly informed of the reasons" without receiving "specific shortcomings or the comments made by the Panel." | You cannot learn enough from a rejection to fix it. Getting the requirements register right the first time is the real defence. |
| **No consistent passing mark; no per-criterion minimum.** Different administering units set different thresholds, or none. | Do not rely on aggregate strength. Serve every criterion. |
| **Eligibility is often taken on trust.** One review found a body "relies on the information provided by the applicant without requiring the submission of any supporting documents", with past non-compliance not automatically barring approval. | Cuts both ways. Under-documented eligibility may pass at assessment — and surface at audit, post-award, when it is far more expensive. |
| **Salary variation is unscrutinised but noticed.** | Benchmark and state the benchmark. |
| **Concurrent workload of named staff is asked about but not carried through to assessment.** Reviews flag this as a gap. | Expect tightening. Answer it accurately now. |
| **Sub-contractor and sub-grantee capability goes unassessed** despite substantial delegated work. | Also a tightening candidate. Evidence your delegates as you would your own team. |
| **Declaration-of-interest administration is weak** — members sitting without filed declarations. | Applicant-side lesson: the probity regime is under active scrutiny and getting stricter, not looser. |

## 17. Reading the absences

What a guideline does **not** say is evidence too.

| Absence | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| No published weightings | Either a rating scale or an unweighted factor list is doing the work. Reconstruct from the form (`SKILL.md`, *Step 1*). Common for intermediaries and discretionary funds. |
| No word or page limits | A business-facing scheme. Allocate by weighting; length is not rewarded. |
| No published panel composition | Common for intermediaries. Assume mixed expertise and a pitch stage. |
| No published rejection reasons | The norm. Almost no body publishes them; the few published ones are compliance failures, not merit failures. |
| No stated language | Ask the secretariat before drafting, not after. |
| No appeal mechanism mentioned | Assume there is none. |
| No stated passing mark | Does not mean there isn't one — see §2.4. |

## 18. Currency

Everything in this file is a **form of words**, not a fact about any live scheme. Schemes are revised, merged, renamed and closed continuously; guidelines carry revision dates and many reserve the administering body's discretion to revise criteria and weightings at any time. Within a single year of compiling the underlying research, several of the schemes it drew on had closed to new applications, been restructured under a new body, or been renamed.

So: use this file to recognise a clause and understand what it obligates. Never use it as the source of a requirement. The current guideline is the only authority, and confirming the scheme is open and the guideline current is Step 0 of every application.
