# Public funding applications

## The genre, and the one thing that drives it

Every body in this genre — a department, a statutory fund, a science park, an incubator, an industry association administering a public scheme, a charitable trust — is spending money it must account for to someone else. A legislature, an auditor, a board, a donor, a parent ministry.

That single fact produces the behaviour you are writing into, and it is the same whether the letterhead says "Commission" or "Corporation":

- The reviewer needs a **defensible** reason to score you high, not a correct one. They are writing a file note that someone may read later.
- They score against a rubric, and they stay inside it. Brilliance outside the rubric does not help.
- They will not do work you left undone. A requirement you made them infer is a requirement they may mark unmet.
- They mostly cannot be appealed, rarely explain a rejection in useful detail, and often cannot be contacted at all during assessment.
- Panels are mixed-expertise by design, precisely so that no single specialist decides. Someone scoring your technical merit may not share your field.

**The governing principle of this skill: never ask a non-specialist to judge the science. Give them something else to judge that is verifiable, external to you, and points the same way** — a measured result against a named baseline, a test report from an accredited lab, a customer letter, a granted patent, a team member's publication record in the exact sub-field. That is how a lay reviewer reaches a high score defensibly, and it is far more durable than prose that sounds impressive.

## Step 1 — reconstruct the rubric

Never write from memory of a scheme, and never assume this year's rules match last year's. Schemes are revised, restructured and closed without notice. Confirm the scheme is open and get the current guideline before drafting a word.

**If criteria and weightings are published**, they are the single most useful instruction in the document. Extract them verbatim, with their exact wording, and allocate narrative length roughly in proportion to weight.

**If they are not published — the common case for intermediaries — reconstruct them, in this order of reliability:**

1. **The application form is the rubric.** Its section headings, their order, and the space or word count allowed for each are the body telling you what it scores and how much it cares. A form with half a page for the technology and three pages for the commercialisation plan is a weighting table.
2. **The fund's stated objectives**, usually in the first page of the guideline or the scheme's founding policy document. Every objective is a criterion whether or not it is listed as one.
3. **Previously funded projects.** The highest-signal document available, and almost always published. Read ten of them. They reveal typical size, typical framing, which sectors actually get funded, and the vocabulary the body uses about its own successes. Mirror that vocabulary.
4. **The body's own annual report or strategic plan** — see *Writing for an intermediary* below.

Write down the reconstructed rubric explicitly and tell the user it is inferred, so they can correct it.

**Then look for hurdles and clocks**, which are easy to miss and fatal to ignore:

- **Per-criterion hurdles.** Some schemes require a pass on *every* criterion, or a minimum fraction of each weighting, or a minimum overall rating below which nothing is funded. Under any of these, a thin section sinks a strong proposal. No criterion may be left under-served for length reasons.
- **Deemed withdrawal.** Many schemes treat an unanswered clarification request as withdrawal after a fixed window — sometimes as short as two weeks, sometimes with no further notice. Others require a recommended applicant to address panel comments within a set period or lose the award. Diarise every clock the moment the guideline is read.

## Step 2 — the requirements register

Before drafting, build a table with one row per requirement: the guideline's **own wording**, the section that will satisfy it, and the evidence annex that proves it. Cover eligibility gates, scored criteria, declarations, format rules, and deadlines.

This register is the spine of all three operating modes. It is also the only defence against the genre's worst property: you will usually not learn enough from a rejection to fix it.

## The core method: two registers, stacked

Every technical claim gets written in four layers, in this order, in the same passage.

1. **Claim** — one plain sentence, 15 to 25 words, no jargon. What it does and why that matters. This is what gets scored.
2. **Mechanism** — one or two plain sentences. How it works, one level of *because*. Still no jargon, but now causal.
3. **Specification** — full technical register. Named methods, exact parameters, standards, tolerances, citations. Not diluted, not padded. This is where accuracy lives, and where a technical reviewer or external assessor looks.
4. **Evidence** — what proves it and where it sits. A measured figure, a prior result, an annex reference.

The non-specialist scores from layers 1 and 2 and can see that 3 and 4 exist and are substantial. The specialist reads 3 and finds it sound. Neither reader is patronised and neither is stranded.

**The integrity test that makes this work:** the claim layer must be a true compression of the specification layer, not a different claim. Read layer 1 and layer 3 side by side — if a specialist would see daylight between them, rewrite layer 1. That gap does not stay hidden. Panels in this genre routinely require applicants to present and answer questions, often require recommended applicants to respond to panel comments before funds are released, and make the application's representations binding under the funding agreement.

## Writing the plain layer

**The so-what ladder.** Climb every technical fact up to a benefit the rubric scores, in at most three steps, then stop.

> Fact: the accumulator uses a residue number system.
> → Mechanism: so the chip adds numbers without carrying digits between them.
> → Benefit: the same calculation finishes on roughly a third of the power.
> → Scored benefit: which is what makes battery-powered deployment viable for small operators. *(Commercial viability — stop here.)*

Stop at the criterion. Climbing past it into world-changing claims costs credibility and invites questions you did not need.

**Analogy discipline.** One analogy per concept, placed *before* the technical statement, never instead of it, drawn from physical everyday experience. Follow it immediately with a re-anchoring sentence: "Technically, this is …". An analogy left standing alone is where a lay reviewer forms a wrong mental model that you then cannot correct.

**Sentence mechanics.** One idea per sentence. Verbs over nominalisations — "we test the coating" beats "coating verification is performed." Expand every acronym at first use *in every major section*, because panel members read sections out of order and often read only the ones assigned to them. Numbers in figures, with units and a comparator: "3.2 seconds, against 11 seconds for the current manual process."

**If the scheme is bilingual**, check whether it accepts either language or requires both, and which version prevails on conflict. Translate the claim layer with particular care — it is the layer that gets scored, and a loose rendering of a precise claim creates exactly the daylight described above.

## Writing the specification layer

Match the complexity of the subject. Not less, and not more.

Understating loses: assessors generally want the novel elements and the underlying principles stated, not assumed. Overstating loses too, and more dangerously — padded complexity reads as evasion to a specialist, it triggers the external technical review most schemes reserve the right to commission, and a reviewer who cannot follow a section does not defer to it. They score it low as insufficiently justified, or refer it out. Panels also read comparatively: next to three proposals a reviewer can follow, the opaque one loses.

**Precision is the authority signal, not density.** "14.2% lower energy per inference than the published MobileNetV3 baseline, across three independent runs (Annex D, Table 2)" carries more expert weight — with both audiences — than a dense paragraph of abstractions. Vagueness is what reads as bluffing. Specificity is what reads as mastery.

So: name the method and cite it; give real parameters and tolerances; state the standard and the testing body; quantify results with sample sizes and variance; and state plainly what is *not* yet known and how the project resolves it. A well-posed open question reads as more scientific than a confident assertion, on every panel.

## Clearing stated requirements

For every row in the register, write one sentence a reviewer can highlight and mark met, with no inference and no help from you.

Pattern: **[the guideline's own term] — [fact with a number, date or name] — [evidence locator]**

> The Applicant has maintained substantive business operation in Hong Kong since its incorporation on 4 March 2019, employing 14 full-time staff based locally, holding an office lease at [address] to 31 December 2028, and having filed tax returns for every year of assessment since 2019/20. (Annex C, pp. 7–19.)

Three rules:

- **Mirror the guideline's vocabulary exactly.** If it says "substantive business operation," do not write "real operations." The reviewer is matching strings against a checklist. Make the match literal.
- **Never make a compliance claim depend on understanding the technology.** "Our architecture is inherently compliant because of X" fails. Compliance claims must resolve to checkable facts: dates, counts, document references, third-party certifications.
- **Apply the highlight test.** Could a reviewer with no technical background find one sentence, highlight it, and write "met" with no further reading? If they would need to synthesise across paragraphs, infer, or take your word for it, rewrite.

## Scope discipline

Answer the question asked, at the altitude asked. Three habits open threads you then have to defend:

- **Rejected alternatives.** Describing approaches you considered and discarded invites "why not that one instead?" State the chosen approach and why it works. Comparison belongs in the innovation section against *external* prior art, not internal options.
- **Hedged future scope.** "We may also explore…" creates scope ambiguity, weakens the deliverables, and causes variation requests later. Deliverables should be concrete and measurable, with parameters indicative of function and performance — near-universal phrasing across this genre.
- **Unprompted risk narration.** Put required risk and contingency content exactly where the form asks, at the depth asked. Do not seed it through the technical narrative.

**Where this stops.** This is about not volunteering *irrelevant* material. It is never about withholding what a scheme requires you to disclose, and in this genre that regime is broad and is itself frequently scored — related-party and procurement relationships, beneficial ownership up to ultimate parent, other funding applied for or held, concurrent workload of named staff, conflicts with nominated assessors, use of AI, regulatory and data-protection compliance. Under-disclosure is the higher-risk failure by a wide margin: it can void an application outright, and it surfaces at audit rather than at assessment. Treat every required disclosure as a persuasive document, written fully and well, not as a form to minimise.

## Winning the subjective criteria

"Innovation," "academic merit," "creativity and quality," "strategic fit" — these are judgment calls, and the temptation is to win them with prose. Prose does not survive a specialist reader or a Q&A. Build the score on an evidence stack:

1. **Prior-art delta.** Name the closest existing approach, cite it, state the measured difference. An unnamed comparison is not a comparison.
2. **Third-party validation.** An accredited test report, a published benchmark, a pilot customer, a letter of intent, a granted or filed patent. External and checkable.
3. **Track record in the exact sub-field.** Publications, patents, prior funded projects of the *named individuals* — not the organisation in general.
4. **A well-posed unknown.** State precisely what is not yet established, and the experiment or milestone that settles it.

This stack is what lets a lay reviewer score merit high without evaluating any science, and what survives external technical review.

**Re-lead the same content for each scheme.** A scheme that prioritises commercialisable outcomes over upstream research wants the deliverable first and the science as justification; a scheme weighted toward the technology wants the reverse; a staged scheme often inverts the emphasis between stages. Never reuse a section across applications without re-leading it to that rubric.

**Fill the optional fields.** Where a form makes team credentials, track record or supporting letters optional, supply them in full. Audit reviews of these schemes repeatedly find most applicants skip optional fields that are nonetheless scored. It is the cheapest differentiator available.

## Writing for an intermediary

Most applications in this genre do not go to a government department. They go to a body the government funds or empowers to distribute money — a science park, a productivity council, an R&D centre, an incubator, a trade development body, an industry association, an arts or film council, a public trust, a corporate foundation. The moderation is the same. Three things change.

- **The body has its own mandate to justify upward, and that mandate is usually the hidden top criterion.** An intermediary must show its parent or board that its portfolio advances the mission it was given. So it needs your project to visibly serve *its* stated purpose, not only the scheme's listed criteria. Find the mandate in its annual report, strategic plan, founding ordinance or sponsoring policy document, and in the language it uses about projects it already funded. Then make the alignment explicit and specific — name the cluster, priority area or strategic objective your project advances, and say concretely how. Specific alignment reads as fit; generic praise of the body reads as filler and is ignored.
- **Less is published, so infer more and say that you inferred.** Criteria, weightings and panel composition are often not published at all. Reconstruct the rubric from the form and the funded-project list as in Step 1, and flag the reconstruction to the user as an assumption to verify.
- **The process is more often a pitch than a paper.** Intermediaries frequently add interviews, presentation sessions and due-diligence rounds. Write so the claim layer survives being spoken aloud and questioned, and prepare the specification layer as answers rather than as text to be read out.

Everything downstream is still government-grade: funding agreements, milestone reporting, audited accounts, procurement and probity rules, and permanent record-keeping. Write the application knowing it becomes a compliance document.

## The compliance interrogation

Ask these of any guideline, whoever issues it. The answers vary; the questions do not.

- **Eligibility of the applicant.** What legal form exactly — incorporated locally, merely registered, non-profit, an institution on a named list? Are listed companies, subsidiaries, or publicly subvented bodies excluded? Is there a local-operations or local-presence test, and what evidence does it name? *(Failure to evidence genuine local operations is one of the few rejection reasons bodies in this genre actually publish.)*
- **Benefit to the jurisdiction.** Is it a scored criterion, a geographic work requirement (a minimum share of work performed locally), an asset lock-in, a local-personnel requirement, or a public-benefit test that excludes benefit to a single private firm? These are different obligations; do not conflate them.
- **Money in.** Matching cash, in-kind contribution, third-party sponsorship, staged ratios? What counts as in-kind and what is expressly excluded — discounts, internal manpower, consultancy and the time-cost of owned equipment are commonly disallowed.
- **Intellectual property.** Who owns arising IP, what licence does the funder take, what happens to background third-party IP, and is IP arrangement itself scored? Where it is scored, write it as an argument, not a clause.
- **No double funding.** Does the bar cover the same *expenditure*, or the same *proposal* submitted concurrently elsewhere? These are very different, and the stricter form catches applicants by surprise.
- **Duration and budget structure.** Project ceiling, extension rules, mandatory cost categories, overhead cap, and the allowable/disallowable expenditure lists. Unspecified items — miscellaneous, sundry, contingency — are commonly rejected outright. Benchmark manpower rates and say what you benchmarked against; unexplained salary variation is a standing audit finding.
- **Related parties and procurement.** Restrictions on engaging associates, relatives, shareholders or connected suppliers; remuneration of shareholders and directors; quotation and tender thresholds; required probity and anti-collusion clauses. This block is where post-award trouble concentrates.
- **Declarations.** Beneficial ownership, conflicts of interest, other applications and grants, concurrent workload, lobbying bars (contacting committee members can void an application), regulatory and national-security compliance, data protection, and disclosure of AI use.
- **Form and language.** Word and page limits, font and margin rules, required annexes, language options and which version prevails, and any required visual summary.

## Failure modes with nothing to do with quality

Build these into the workflow, not just the document. They lose fundable applications.

- **Deemed withdrawal on silence** — an unanswered clarification, or panel comments not addressed in time.
- **Late-discovered eligibility gates** — found after drafting, when nothing can be done.
- **Unfilled optional fields** that were scored anyway.
- **No appeal.** Most bodies in this genre make the decision final. Assume you get one attempt.
- **Thin rejection feedback**, which is the norm, so plan resubmission as the recourse and treat getting the register right the first time as the real defence.
- **Resubmission is a designed process, not a resend.** Where permitted it usually requires substantial revision, explicit response to the stated rejection reasons, new evidence, and often a written statement of what changed. Inadequately revised resubmissions are commonly returned unprocessed.

## Operating modes

**Draft.** Guideline → reconstructed rubric → requirements register → weight-proportioned outline → sections in two registers → compliance sentences → evidence annex list → self-audit against the register. Write the highest-weighted section first.

**Rewrite.** Read the existing draft against the register before changing a word. Report what is missing, what is under-weighted for its rubric share, what fails the highlight test, and where the claim and specification layers have drifted apart. Then rewrite, preserving the applicant's factual content exactly — never introduce a fact, figure, date or credential the user has not supplied. Mark any gap needing a fact you do not have as `[NEEDS: …]` and ask.

**Check.** Produce a table: requirement (guideline's wording) | where addressed | verdict (met / arguable / not addressed) | what would make it unarguable. Sort by risk: hurdles and eligibility gates first, then declarations, then scored criteria by weight. Be blunt about "arguable" — that is the category that loses applications, and the applicant cannot learn it from a rejection letter.

## Two things this skill will not do

Both because they lose, and because the money is public.

**It will not inflate technical complexity to deter scrutiny.** Complexity in the specification layer is exactly the complexity the subject has. Manufactured density does not intimidate a panel into deference — it triggers external review, scores low as insufficiently justified, and reads as evasion to the one reviewer who does understand the field.

**It will not let the plain layer claim more than the specification supports.** The layers must compress, not diverge. Panels require presentations and questions, recommended applicants must usually answer panel comments before funds are released, and everything in the application becomes binding under the funding agreement. A gap engineered into the document surfaces at the moment it is most expensive.

Where the user asks for either, offer the evidence stack instead — it produces the same score, and it holds up.
